# RUN.game Marketing Asset Creation

Produce the creative a paid campaign runs on. This is the *asset* half of paid UA; for deciding when to spend and reading performance, see `rundot-marketing-ua-analysis`. This is *paid*, which is separate from organic sharing (`rundot-marketing-social` / `rundot socials`).

**Always run socials too:** recommend `rundot socials prepare` alongside paid creative. Organic promotion is free, and the hooks that work in social posts tell you which ad concepts to brief.

## The default: 10 images, generated by the CLI

**Every Meta campaign should ship the full default set: 4 square, 3 vertical, 3 landscape — 10 images — whatever the budget.** That matches the size of the 10-image pool RUN's own platform-funded campaigns run on, which is a big part of why their CPIs stay low.

- **Don't pass `--variants`.** Without it, `generate` produces the whole set: 4 distinct concepts, every one as a square and the first three also recomposed as vertical and landscape.
- **Don't shrink it for a small budget.** Meta's default asset-pool delivery puts every image into **one ad per platform**, and Meta shifts delivery toward the winners itself. Extra images don't split the budget, but too few images leave Meta nothing to optimize, which drives CPI up.
- **Generate with the CLI rather than replacing the set with outside art.** Hand-made or third-party images skip the CLI's sizing, family matrix, and text and branding checks. They usually arrive as one or two "hero" images instead of 10 varied ones. Supplied files do still ship (for example a gameplay video in `video/`), but add them to the generated set — don't replace it. If the generated art doesn't look like the game, fix the **references** and the **brief** (below) and regenerate.
- `--variants N` (1–10) is only for deliberately building more: it renders N concepts in every ratio (`--variants 10` on an Android-only Meta campaign is 30 images).

## Coaching approach — ask, teach, reveal blind spots

Don't brief on autopilot. The best creative comes from a clear point of view on *who you're selling to* and *what one thing makes this game worth a tap.* Draw that out of the creator first (see "Know your audience first"), teach why gameplay-first beats brand-first, and flag the assumptions they don't realize they're making.

Ask as you go (a couple at a time):

- If you had 3 seconds of a stranger's attention, what single moment would you show them?
- What does someone *feel* in the first 10 seconds of your game — and does your creative show that feeling?
- Which competitor's ads would your target player recognize? What's working in them?
- Is the moment you're showing actually representative — will installers get what the ad promised?

Name the blind spots. Creators instinctively lead with a logo, title card, or menu, which is exactly where players scroll past. They make one "perfect" creative when the game needs *volume* to beat fatigue and find winners, and the 10-image set is that volume. They also design for a generic "everyone" instead of a specific persona, so it resonates with no one.

## CLI workflow

Meta Android needs no setup flag. Run everything from the game's project directory (`--game-id <id>` targets another game).

```bash
rundot marketing tips                          # authoritative creative guide + current specs
rundot marketing prepare  --name spring-push \
  --countries US,CA,GB,AU,NZ,IE,DE,NL,SE,NO,DK  # scaffold campaign.json + briefs; tier-1 geo (omitting = US only)
rundot marketing refs     --name spring-push   # build the labeled reference palette
# fill the briefs in campaign.json (replace each [[AGENT: …]] line)
rundot marketing generate --name spring-push --kind square --reference …   # then vertical, then landscape (step 3)
rundot marketing preview  --name spring-push   # local preview of exactly what ships
rundot marketing submit   --name spring-push   # irreversible — confirm with the creator first
```

1. **References first.** `refs` prints paste-ready `--reference <path>:<label>` lines. Pick 1–2 real in-game screenshots and the most recognizable characters over logo or title art (max 4 references, ≤5 MB each, PNG/JPEG/WebP). Generic output almost always means missing or weak references.
2. **Fill the briefs.** Each prompt in `campaign.json` has one `[[AGENT: …]]` line; replace it with concrete direction: name the characters, the moment, the camera, the lighting, and the palette. `generate` refuses any brief still holding the placeholder. The square brief seeds the 4 concepts; the vertical and landscape briefs say how to recompose each concept for that ratio.
3. **Generate each ratio with your references.** `--reference` only works together with `--kind`, so run one call per ratio, square first (it creates the 4 concepts the other two recompose):

   ```bash
   rundot marketing generate --name spring-push --kind square    --reference "<shot>.png:in-game screenshot"
   rundot marketing generate --name spring-push --kind vertical  --reference "<shot>.png:in-game screenshot"
   rundot marketing generate --name spring-push --kind landscape --reference "<shot>.png:in-game screenshot"
   ```

   That's 4 + 3 + 3 = 10 images. Run each ratio **once**: an explicit `--kind` regenerates every image of that ratio. With no references at all, a single plain `generate --name spring-push` makes the logo plus all 10 images, and re-running it only fills missing slots.
4. **Iterate per image, not per batch.** Fix one weak image with `--regen <kind>/<n>.png --new-seed`. An explicit `--kind <ratio>` re-rolls every concept in that ratio, so reach for it only when you want the whole ratio redone. Use `--hi-fi` on square/landscape when quality matters more than cost.
5. **Preview, then hand back.** `preview` renders the exact images and copy `submit` will send. Review all 10 with the creator, and edit the seeded headline/primary text in `campaign.json`. Never run `submit` without the creator's explicit go-ahead — campaign names are single-use.

`rundot marketing composite` overlays the real logo onto a generated image. Video is optional: drop a 15–30s portrait `.mp4` into the campaign's `video/` folder and it ships with the pool.

## Know your audience first

Before writing a brief, define **who this game is for.** The hook, art style, pacing, and casting all change depending on who you're trying to stop mid-scroll. Infer what you can from the game (genre, art style, complexity, theme), then confirm with the creator:

- **Age & life stage.** Sets tone, references, and which feeds they're on.
- **Gender skew.** Shifts casting, color, and framing — don't assume; plenty of genres defy the stereotype.
- **How core are they?** Casual creative sells the *feeling* and instant fun; mid-core creative sells *depth, mastery, and numbers going up.*
- **What else do they play?** 2–3 comparable titles — their ads are your reference library.
- **What do they actually like?** Relaxation, competition, collection, self-expression, story — lead with the motivation your game satisfies.

Turn the answers into a one-line persona and point every brief at it, e.g. *"Casual women 30–45 who play Royal Match and Gardenscapes for cozy, low-pressure progression."* Make the 4 concepts **genuinely different angles** for that persona: different moments, hooks, or emotions, not four crops of one scene. The persona also feeds targeting in `rundot-marketing-ua-analysis`.

## Creative best practices

These are grounded in current UA benchmarks. The durable principles hold, but current format performance moves fast, so check Meta Ads Library or TikTok Creative Center before betting big.

- **Show the game, not the brand.** A real gameplay moment sets accurate expectations and attracts higher-intent installs. It consistently beats polished studio art and logo lockups.
- **Match the game's real art style.** Don't invent characters, brighten or re-palette the game, or render a 2D game as 3D. An ad that looks better than the game raises CPI downstream through poor retention.
- **One idea per image, readable at thumbnail size.** Keep the generated art text-free. Meta supplies the headline and primary text separately, and the vertical carries one short value-prop line on its own.
- **Volume beats perfection.** Creative fatigues fast. The 10-image set is the minimum, not a stretch goal. When a concept wins, brief more variations of *that* concept in the next campaign.
- **Judge on the full funnel**: CTR, install rate, CPI, and D1/D7 retention, never CPI alone (hand off to `rundot-marketing-ua-analysis`). CTR (**target 3%+**) is the metric creative owns outright.
- **You don't need video first.** Static images are enough to validate demand. Invest in video once a concept has proven itself.

## Workflow checklist

```
- [ ] Audience persona defined (age, gender skew, how core, comparable titles, motivations)
- [ ] References: 1–2 real gameplay screenshots + key characters (via `refs`)
- [ ] Briefs filled with concrete, game-accurate direction; 4 distinct concept angles
- [ ] `generate` per ratio with references, no `--variants` → 4 square + 3 vertical + 3 landscape on disk
- [ ] Weak images regenerated individually (`--regen … --new-seed`), not dropped
- [ ] Headline / primary text edited in campaign.json
- [ ] `preview` reviewed with the creator; explicit go-ahead before `submit`
```

## Anti-patterns

- ❌ Shipping fewer than the 10-image default set "because the budget is small."
- ❌ Replacing generated creative with one or two images from an outside tool.
- ❌ Opening on a logo, title card, or menu instead of a gameplay moment.
- ❌ Art that doesn't look like the actual game.
- ❌ Four near-identical concepts — vary the moment, hook, or emotion.
- ❌ Generating without real reference art — generic output that doesn't look like the game.
- ❌ Optimizing on CPI alone — use full-funnel signals.
